Efficiency Frontiers

MoE Router

Expert routing simulator: capacity factor, token dropping, expert parallelism

1The problem

A mixture-of-experts (MoE) layer promises the capacity of a huge model at the compute cost of a small one: each token is processed by only top_k of E experts. In practice the router must dispatch tokens to experts that live on different devices, and each expert has a fixed per-step capacity. When too many tokens pick the same popular expert, the overflow is silently dropped — those tokens skip the expert entirely. Dropping is the hidden tax of MoE: it never appears in the parameter count, but it directly reduces the tokens that receive full expert computation.

2The current gap

Most MoE discussions focus on gating (top-k softmax) and on the parameter savings. What is rarely quantified in one place is the three-way tradeoff that actually governs MoE serving:

  1. Capacity factor (CF) sets `expert_capacity = ceil(CF * n_tokens / n_experts)`. CF < 1 under-provisions; CF > 1 wastes memory and compute on padding.
  2. Load balancing (auxiliary loss) decides whether capacity is spread evenly or piled onto a few popular experts.
  3. Expert parallelism replaces the drop problem with a communication problem: all-to-all dispatch/combine volume that does not shrink as you add devices.

3How the solution addresses it

A numpy-only simulator (src/llm_inference_engine/moe_router/) with three pieces:

  • gating.py — topk_gate (softmax + top-k + renormalize), noisy_gate (logit noise to simulate imperfect routing), balanced_gate (models the steady-state effect of an aux load-balancing loss as reduced logit skew).
  • router.py — route dispatches tokens in order, drops assignments beyond expert_capacity, and reports drop rate, per-expert counts, imbalance ratio (max/mean), and processed tokens.
  • parallel.py — shard_experts, alltoall_bytes (n_tokens top_k d_model bytes_per_elem 2, dispatch + combine), device_load (per-device compute slots and comm share).

Experiments are seeded (np.random.default_rng(51)); two consecutive runs produce identical headlines, charts, and results/results.md.

6Insights

  1. Dropping is the hidden tax of MoE. With realistic skewed expert popularity, the default CF=1.0 throws away 61% of top-1 expert assignments. The parameter-count story ("only 2 of 8 experts active") says nothing about how many tokens actually got expert compute.
  2. **Capacity factor trades memory/compute for quality, with diminishing returns.** Raising CF from 1.0 to 2.0 costs 2x the expert activation memory/compute but only cuts top-1 drops from 61% to 38% under skew. Throwing capacity at a routing problem is expensive and incomplete.
  3. Load balancing beats raw capacity. The aux-loss-idealized router achieves at CF=1.0 (9.3% drops) what skewed gating cannot reach even at CF=2.0 (38.1% drops) — and it does so without any extra memory. This is why production MoE training treats the balancing loss as first-class.
  4. top-k multiplies the capacity bill. Balanced top-2 routing is perfectly even yet still drops 50% at CF=1.0, because each token books two expert slots. Capacity planning must be done per assignment, not per token.
  5. Expert parallelism converts the drop problem into a comm problem. Sharding 16 experts over 8 devices leaves only 1024 compute slots per device but still moves 128 MiB of all-to-all traffic per step — the network, not the experts, becomes the scaling bottleneck.
